# why-are-we-here

Coming from a background in Design Management and creative Planning, I have always viewed design as an exercise in turning abstract concepts into tangible experiences. However, throughout my practice, I constantly hit an invisible ceiling: technical execution.
When designing my undergraduate capstone project which is an indie game, I lacked technical proficiency in game engines and 3D modeling. To deliver the project on time, I strategically worked around my limitations. I opted for 2D graphics over 3D, and built the game within RPG Maker instead of a custom engine. While this compromise allowed me to complete the assignment, it taught me a frustrating lesson: when you do not understand the underlying technology of your medium, your tools end up defining your creative boundaries rather than your original vision.
In the era of AI, many people believe that code generation tools can solve this problem. If a model can generate code from simple descriptions, it seems like we no longer need to learn how to write it. However, relying purely on text prompts creates new problems. When the generated code produces unexpected bugs or fails to meet specific design needs, a designer who cannot read code is completely helpless. Prompting without understanding code logic is like trying to guide a team in a language you do not speak. To truly control a design, reading and understanding code is far more important than just typing prompts.
AI as a Tutor, Not a Creator. Reflecting on my capstone project, when faced with technical limitations, my first instinct was not to hand over the creative process to AI. Instead, I strived to create within the bounds of what I could master, choosing RPG Maker as a deliberate bridge for my ideas. To me, AI should serve two distinct purposes: accelerating workflow once a skill is already mastered, or acting as a personalized tutor when venturing into unknown territory—much like how I am navigating this course right now. Without understanding the underlying logic of code, blindly relying on AI strip a designer of their agency. My undergraduate project, though technically unpolished, remains immensely meaningful to me because every constraint was negotiated by my own choices. It marked the crucial first step toward my dream career. If I had simply let AI generate it, the work would have lost its soul. I am learning to code not to outsource my thinking, but to preserve my agency as the true author of my work.
Through this course, my goal is not to become a professional programmer overnight, but to build a strong foundation. I want to understand how software works from the inside out so I can communicate better with developers and AI tools alike. By learning the basics of programming, I can bridge the gap between creative ideas and practical implementation, making sure my projects remain grounded in sound logic.
Ultimately, learning to code is not about transforming into a software engineer or abandoning design intuition. It is about expanding the canvas of what is possible. Tools will continue to evolve, and AI models will inevitably become more capable, but the vision and emotional core of a project must come from the designer. So as I said, I am here to expand the range of tools I can use to achieve my goals. Learning to code gives me the power to define my own creative boundaries. It allows me to step beyond working around limitations and become a creator who truly shapes the work.
Learning programming is not about abandoning my design management background to become a full-time software engineer, nor is it about blindly chasing technical perfection. Looking ahead, I do not need every personal project I create to be technically flawless. What matters most to me is that every work I produce retains its soul—just like my very first project did.
